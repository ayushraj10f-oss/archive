Huan Tsang (Xuanzang) records that Indian administrative practice maintained separate custodians for archives and official records, with annals and state papers collectively referred to as ni-lo-pi-t’u (restored by Julien as Nilapita, “dark-blue store”). Related evidence from Bana’s Harshacharita and its analysis by V. S. Agrawala suggests that a systematic cadastral land survey may have been conducted during the Gupta period. Land revenue appears to have been assessed according to plough-units (sira), with the number of such units attached to village names in official records. These figures seem to have remained in use into the 7th century under Emperor Harsha. Sources: Harshacharita; The Deeds of Harsha; V. S. Agrawala, Critical Analysis of Bana.

  27. H. R. Harrop, a civil official encouraged by several British scholars, assembled a team to test classical Hindu architecture by reconstructing a mansion based on the Mānasāra. P. K. Acharya, who was chosen for the project, built the Svastika Mansion in Prayagraj. Acharya himself departed from certain Mānasāra prescriptions and argued that śāstra should be followed in spirit rather than mechanically in letter. Blindly applying cardinal orientations appropriate to the text’s original geographical context would produce poorly ventilated and badly lit buildings in northern India. This represents a form of scientific traditionalism: Mānasāra principles were developed in a southern climatic environment with different wind directions and sunlight patterns. Applying them uncritically in the north would produce impractical architecture. The Mānasāra series edited by P. K. Acharya might have served as a foundation for architectural development in post-1947 Indian public buildings, but this possibility was largely neglected.

  44. Udayana’s Nine Proofs for the existence of Īśvara
    Nyāyakusumāñjali (PDF)

    1. Kāryatvāt — Because the world is an effect
    Every composite effect requires an intelligent maker. The universe is the largest effect. It requires the largest maker — omniscient, omnipotent. That is Īśvara.

    2. Āyojanāt — Because atoms were combined
    Atoms are inert and cannot combine themselves. The first act of combination required an intentional agent who knew what to make and how. That agent is Īśvara.

    3. Dhṛteḥ — Because the world is sustained
    The cosmic order does not collapse. Ongoing stable sustenance requires ongoing conscious upholding. That sustainer is Īśvara.

    4. Saṃharaṇāt — Because dissolution is ordered
    Cosmic dissolution happens systematically rather than chaotically. Ordered withdrawal requires a directing intelligence. That dissolver is Īśvara.

    5. Padāt — Because language has meaning
    Word–meaning relations are conventional rather than natural. Conventions must be established and taught. The first humans had no prior teacher; therefore someone outside the human chain established the original semantic relations. That establisher is Īśvara.

    6. Pratyayāt — Because of universal human cognition
    The intuition of a supreme being appears across cultures and eras. Cognition requires an object; a universal cognition pointing to nothing is inexplicable. Its object is Īśvara.

    7. Śruteḥ — Because of scriptural testimony
    The Vedic texts unanimously testify to Īśvara. In Nyāya epistemology, testimony from reliable speakers is a valid pramāṇa. Their object is Īśvara.

    8. Anvayataḥ — Because moral order is maintained
    Karma reliably produces appropriate results across all beings and times. This requires an omniscient administrator who tracks every action and delivers every consequence. That administrator is Īśvara.

    9. Saṃkhyāviśeṣāt — Because number had to be imparted
    Number is not intrinsic to things but produced by cognition. Ordered creation required specific numerical arrangements of atoms. No finite mind could cognize all atoms simultaneously; an omniscient mind whose cognition imparted number to all atoms at once must exist. That mind is Īśvara.

    Example — Canto 20: Description of Madras (Cannappaṭṭaṇa)

    Sanskrit:
    अथ चन्नपट्टण(मद्रास)वर्णनम् २०॰
    इति विमानमन्यतः प्रधापयन्मतो दृष्ट्वा—
    एषा कैरविणी विशल्यकरणी दोषाशुगार्तात्मना-
    मेत्द्रोघिस पार्थेसारिथिरिति रूयातः सिमन्धे हरिः ॥
    यत्पादाम्बुजचुम्बिनां सुरपतेः कोटीरगारुत्मत-
    स्तोमानां तुलसीदलप्रकरतो नो वेद भेदं जनः ॥ २६०॥

    “Thus viewing the city from the aerial vehicle while passing — here Viṣṇu, known as Pārthasārathi (the charioteer of Arjuna), residing at the Triplicane shrine, is the remover of the arrows of faults for those afflicted by them. For those who kiss His lotus feet, people know no distinction between the heaps of gems on Indra’s crown and a cluster of tulasī leaves.”

    Kṛśānu then launches into a severe critique of the “Hūṇas,” accusing them of failing on two axes simultaneously: ritual-social order and bodily purity, both of which fall under the broader Sanskritic concept of śauca.

    Kṛśānu’s Verdict on Madras — The Hūṇa Problem

    Sanskrit:
    कुत्सिततमामेवं स्पष्टयति—हूणा इति । करुण्या दयया हीनाः हूणाः । ब्राह्मण-गणं माझनसमुदायं तृणवत् तृणसिव न गणयन्ति । तुच्छोक्वैन्तीत्यर्थः । किञ्च ये हूणाः शौचं पुरीषोत्सगोनन्तरं गुदक्षालनस्थानक्षमापि न आचरन्ति, किमुत मृज्जलदिग्धा स्नानादिरूपं, अत एव तेषां दोषाः पापातिः पारे वाचां वाचा वक्तुमशक्या इत्यर्थः ॥ २६२ ॥

    “The major fault Kṛśānu identifies clearly is this: the Hūṇas lack compassion. They do not regard the assembly of Brahmins even as straw. Moreover they do not even practice washing the posterior after relieving themselves, let alone the fuller practices of bathing with water and clay. Their faults are therefore beyond the power of words to enumerate.”

    Sanskrit:
    शौच्यागिषु हूणकादिषु धनं शिष्टेषु च क्षिष्टाम्
    दुर्मेधसु धरापिपत्यमतुलं दक्षेषु मिक्षाटनम् ॥
    लावण्यं ललनासु दुष्कुलभवास्व्यासु नीरूपताम्

  111. A discussion preserved in the Brāhmaṇa exegetical tradition attributes to Śyāparṇa Sāyakāyana a pointed challenge to the logic of sacrifice. If the yajña is truly an offering of the self, why does the sacrificer not place his own body on the altar? Why is another being killed in his place? The question is not rhetorical but structural: what is the sacrificer actually giving?

    Śyāparṇa’s resolution is that the act has not been avoided, only displaced. The animal is not an external victim but a substitute body. The sacrificer enters the ritual through identification: he is the sacrifice, and the victim is himself in operative form. What appears as the killing of another is, within the logic of the rite, a controlled and repeatable self-offering. Without substitution, sacrifice would terminate the sacrificer; with it, the act can be performed, transferred, and renewed.

    “puruṣo vai yajñaḥ” — Man is the sacrifice. (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 2.1)
    “The animal is a substitute for man.” (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 3.1.2, substitution doctrine)

  149. Congress as “Stranger King”: a structural reading

    Manu Joseph’s argument (2019) that the Gandhi family persists as the “second-best choice” within Congress factions can be extended beyond party dynamics into a broader structural pattern. India’s political field is marked by extreme internal fragmentation — caste, region, language, sect — each with durable loyalties and zero-sum competition in a democratic setting. In such a landscape, no single endogenous bloc can easily dominate without provoking consolidation against it. This creates space for what anthropologist Marshall Sahlins termed the “Stranger King”: an authority perceived as external to internal rivalries and therefore capable of arbitrating between them. Historically, such figures derive power precisely from their distance from local factional identities. The Congress Party approximates this role. Unlike formations identified with specific regions, castes, or linguistic blocs, it presents itself as structurally unanchored. The Gandhi leadership, by genealogy and political positioning, reinforces this perception of detachment. This allows Congress to function as a broker of competing interests rather than a representative of any one of them. In this reading, Congress is not neutral in substance but neutral in perception — and that perception is sufficient. Groups that feel excluded by more clearly rooted political formations periodically return to it, not out of ideological alignment but because it appears as an external balancing force within a system otherwise defined by internal competition. The persistence of Congress, despite repeated organizational decline, is better explained through this structural role than through electoral performance alone.

  196. Bhīṣma Pitāmah on the nature of women

    Prefatory note — on the authority of the source: The weight given to Bhīṣma's discourses in the Mahābhārata is not incidental. Śrī Vāsudeva himself instructs Yudhiṣṭhira (Śānti Parva, Chapter 54, verses 14–16) to learn from Bhīṣma before he departs:

    "O lord of the earth, enquire of him about the four branches of knowledge — morality, profit, pleasure, and salvation — about the sacrifices and the rites laid down for the four orders, about the four modes of life, and about the kingly duties in full. When Bhīṣma, that foremost one of Kuru's race, will disappear from the world, every kind of knowledge will disappear with him."

    This is not a peripheral figure being cited — Kṛṣṇa himself designates Bhīṣma as the supreme repository of all knowledge across every domain. Whatever Bhīṣma says on any subject carries this framing. The discourses that follow in Śānti Parva and Anuśāsana Parva are presented as the last transmission of a total civilizational inheritance.

    I. The cosmological claim — Śānti Parva 12.187.40:

    स्त्रियः स्वभावतः क्षेत्रं पुरुषाः क्षेत्रज्ञाः गुणैः
    striyaḥ svabhāvataḥ kṣetraṃ puruṣāḥ kṣetrajñāḥ guṇaiḥ

    "By their nature, women are Kṣetra [the Field]; men are Kṣetrajña [the Knower of the Field] in respect of attributes/qualities."

    The terms carry their full Bhagavad Gītā weight (BG 13.1–2): Kṣetra is inert matter, the site of action and consequence; Kṣetrajña is the sentient witnessing principle. The verse maps the male-female polarity onto the foundational Sāṃkhya dyad — Prakṛti (field, feminine) and Puruṣa (consciousness, masculine). This is a claim about the metaphysical character of femaleness and maleness as such, not about social roles or individual persons. The guṇaiḥ qualification specifies the domain — the realm of conditioned qualities — but does not soften the claim within that domain. It is flat and unqualified within its scope.

    II. The legal-social claim — Anuśāsana Parva, Section XLV (Bhīṣma citing Sukratu, grandson of Janaka):

    "There is the well-known declaration of the scriptures that women are incompetent to enjoy freedom at any period of their life. If this were not the path trodden by the righteous, how could this scriptural declaration exist?"

    The section is framed around a question from Yudhiṣṭhira on dowry and marriage. Bhīṣma cites Sukratu — the grandson of Janaka of Videha — as the authority for this position. Sukratu's framing is important: the argument is not merely a social prescription but is grounded in śruti-authority and the conduct of the righteous as self-validating norms. The passage continues to address the daughter's status in inheritance (equal to the son in certain conditions), sale of daughters as sin, and the Āsura form of marriage — revealing that the discourse on women is simultaneously legal, economic, and cosmological, not reducible to any one register.

    III. The oscillation problem and the editorial question:

    The Anuśāsana Parva does not hold a single consistent position on women. Within the same parva attributed to the same speaker, one finds: "woman is poison, she is fire, she is the sharp edge of the razor" (Section XL); "women are incompetent to enjoy freedom at any period of their life" (Section XLV, citing Sukratu); and elsewhere: "women should always be worshipped and treated with affection — there where women are treated with respect, the very deities are said to be filled with joy." These are not reconcilable positions within a single coherent theology. The violent oscillation is itself textual evidence of layered composition. The Anuśāsana Parva is among the most editorially unstable portions of the epic — scholars including V.S. Sukthankar in the BORI critical edition project identified the sections on women's duties and social customs as showing the most severe manuscript inconsistencies, with substantial variation across recensions.

    IV. What can be said with confidence:

    The kṣetra-kṣetrajña verse makes a cosmological claim about sex that is philosophically coherent within Sāṃkhya metaphysics and consistent with the Gītā's framework. The Sukratu citation makes a legal-social claim about female freedom that is presented as śruti-grounded and beyond contestation. These two claims are not identical but they are not contradictory — both subordinate femaleness to a structuring masculine principle, one at the cosmological level and one at the social-legal level. The reverential passages elsewhere in the same parva do not cancel these — they indicate that the text holds women as simultaneously Prakṛti (to be known/witnessed), as legally dependent, and as ritually potent and worthy of honour. These three positions coexist because they operate in different registers. None of the three can be dismissed as interpolation without evidence; none can be elevated as the text's "true" position without special pleading.

    Mahābhārata 12.187.40 (Śānti Parva, Mokṣadharma); Mahābhārata 13.45 (Anuśāsana Parva, Section XLV — Bhīṣma citing Sukratu); Mahābhārata 12.54.14–16 (Śānti Parva — Kṛṣṇa on Bhīṣma's authority); Bhagavad Gītā 13.1–2 (Kṣetra-Kṣetrajña framework); V.S. Sukthankar et al., BORI Critical Edition of the Mahābhārata

  197. The waning of Hindu curiosity about comets

    Early Hindu astronomers maintained serious and systematic interest in comets — classifying them, recording appearances, and debating their nature. The Bṛhat Saṃhitā of Varāhamihira preserves three competing school positions: Parāśara held there were 101 Ketus; Garga counted 1000; Nārada declared there was only one Ketu reappearing in different forms and places. Varāhamihira dismissed the metaphysical dispute pragmatically: the effects ought to be declared through position, conjunction, colour, and smoky mantle regardless. Whether Nārada's single-Ketu theory reflects some transmitted knowledge of a periodic comet — Halley's being the obvious candidate — cannot be determined. The typological system classified comets by colour (agni-putra-ketu for a greenish comet), tracked ketu-grahayuddha (cometary war with planets), and maintained specific omens: one type was said to cause the death of the lord of the Śūrasenas. Critically, the old Hindu tradition appears to have understood comets as celestial reflecting-light objects — ahead of the Aristotelian framework in which comets remained atmospheric phenomena until Geminiano Montanari demonstrated otherwise in 1664. Manasataramgini, observing Comet C/2022 E3 (ZTF) in February 2023 — greenish, in near-conjunction with Mars — noted the loss directly: "One of the character flaws of the later day H was the loss of interest in comets. Early H astronomers in contrast had considerable interest in comets."

The “chump” is not merely someone who loses out economically, but someone who followed the rules—saved, worked, complied—only to discover that these very behaviors are being exploited by a system that redistributes benefits toward those who did not.   224. In-group Bias in the Indian Judiciary

    Large-scale study (~5 million criminal cases) finds minimal evidence of systematic in-group bias by caste, religion, or gender among Indian judges. The only measurable effect appears in cases involving rare, shared surnames, suggesting localized affinity rather than broad structural discrimination.

    Source: Elliott Ash et al., "In-group Bias in the Indian Judiciary: Evidence from 5 Million Criminal Cases" (2022)

    The paper examines an unusually large dataset of criminal cases to test whether judges favour defendants who share their caste, religion, or gender. The headline result is negative: in most cases, these shared identities have no statistically significant impact on outcomes.

    The only consistent effect appears when judge and defendant share a rare surname. Even here, the pattern is one of limited in-group affinity rather than generalized out-group hostility. Where names differ, there is no corresponding negative effect in the data.

    This sharply diverges from broader claims about pervasive caste bias operating through shared surnames. The study’s results suggest that such effects, if present, are far more localized and contingent than commonly assumed.

    One of the more interesting aspects of the paper is methodological. The authors construct a large labelled dataset of Indian names with associated characteristics and use it to train a neural network to probabilistically identify caste, religion, and gender. This allows classification at scale across millions of cases, rather than relying on coarse or self-reported categories.

    Findings of this kind strengthen the case for a judiciary that is both powerful and politically insulated. If large-scale evidence suggests that judges are not systematically dispensing outcomes along caste, religious, or gender lines, then the argument for subjecting judicial appointments and functioning to greater political control weakens. Institutional independence, rather than representational engineering, appears to be the more reliable safeguard. This informs my own aversion to proposals like the NJAC and my relief at judicial pushback against attempts at greater executive or regulatory encroachment, including in areas such as UGC oversight.

  225. U.S. Joint Chiefs of Staff on Zionist Strategy (Early 1948)

  226. Nietzsche on Buddhism vs Christianity

    Source: The Antichrist (1888), by Friedrich Nietzsche

    Nietzsche draws a sharp and sustained contrast between the two religions, not as parallel moral systems but as fundamentally different psychological and civilizational responses to suffering. Buddhism, in his account, is a religion of clarity: it replaces “sin” with “suffering,” rejects metaphysical consolation, and adopts what he calls a “hygienic” approach to life—minimizing pain, eliminating ressentiment, and avoiding emotional excess. It is “beyond good and evil,” not in a celebratory sense, but in the sense of having moved past moralistic distortions altogether.

    Christianity, by contrast, is presented as a system that moralizes suffering and weaponizes it. It elevates guilt, resentment, and self-denial into central virtues, sustains emotional dependence through concepts like sin and grace, and directs hostility outward toward strength, hierarchy, and vitality. Where Buddhism ends enmity by dissolving it, Christianity preserves and intensifies it under moral cover.

    The distinction is not merely doctrinal but physiological and psychological. Buddhism emerges, in Nietzsche’s telling, from a refined, late civilizational condition—one marked by sensitivity, introspection, and the need to manage suffering without illusion. Christianity, on the other hand, is described as arising among more brutalized populations, incorporating harsher, more coercive elements in order to organize and control them.

    The result is two very different structures. One seeks to reduce suffering through detachment, moderation, and the elimination of reactive emotions. The other sustains itself through the continual activation of those same emotions—guilt, hope, fear, and resentment—binding the individual to a moral and theological framework that demands both submission and belief.
