Gandhi, my father

2007
Director: Feroz Abbas Khan
Writer: Feroz Abbas Khan
Cast: Akshaye Khanna, Shefali Shah, Darshan Jariwala

Gandhi, my father starts off as a rare and interesting film exploring the private life of one of the most public men in history, but eventually devolves into Attenborough imitation and hagiography, abandoning its own premise halfway through.

Positives

  1. Akshaye Khanna as Harilal Gandhi: Akshaye Khanna delivers a performance with remarkable internalisation and subtlety. Harilal is insecure, resentful, self-destructive, but never cartoonish and almost always worthy of sympathy. Khanna portrays Harilal not as a tantrum-prone rebel but as a believer who becomes disillusioned through neglect and comparison. His performance is the moral centre of the film (even though the script sidelines him later on).

  2. Character writing in the first half: The first hour of the film explores a rarely discussed aspect of Gandhi: moral absolutism, noble in isolation, but destructive in intimate relationships. This is done without turning him into a villain. He is never portrayed as cruel or cold, but as principled to a fault, emotionally unavailable and unintentionally devastating as a father. The depiction of virtue curdling into familial neglect is mature and the film’s strongest aspect.

  3. Cinematography: The film is visually elegant throughout. Period-accurate sets, impeccable lighting, and careful composition give the film a distinct texture. Even when the writing falters later on, the visuals never do.

  4. Screenplay competence (as a technicality): The dialogue is largely natural and, in the first half, layered and restrained.

Negatives

  1. Abandonment of theme and protagonist: Halfway through the film, Harilal ceases to function as a joint protagonist. He appears sporadically, seemingly to satisfy an obligation rather than to advance a coherent arc. The film, titled Gandhi, my father, was supposed to depict Gandhi as a father from Harilal’s point of view. Instead, it morphs into a highlight reel of Gandhian movements, speeches, and moral triumphs; a pale imitation of Gandhi (1982).

  2. Ideological insulation and narrative cowardice: At a certain point, the film appears afraid of its own critique. Having set up a compelling examination of how Gandhi’s moral absolutism damages his relationship with his son, the film grows anxious about the implications of portraying Gandhi as a flawed father. Instead of trusting the audience, it retreats into ideological insulation.

    This insulation manifests as an unnecessary scene depicting Gandhi being attacked by fanatical Hindu opponents. The scene has no bearing on the father–son dynamic, advances no character arc, and contributes nothing to Harilal’s story. Its purpose is symbolic: to ensure that Gandhi’s critics are framed not as political opponents, but as violent, regressive, and morally irredeemable.

    The historical framing is revealing. While a bombing attempt on Gandhi in Poona did occur, the film attributes it to casteist fanaticism. In reality, opposition to Gandhi at the time was driven largely by disagreements over appeasement and political concessions, a far more nuanced and a less morally absolute conflict. By collapsing this into inaccurate casteist caricatures, the film converts political disagreement into absolute moral evil.

    This becomes especially conspicuous when contrasted with Harilal’s conversion arc. His emotionally coerced conversion to Islam is framed in a tragic and sociological register, while the bombing is framed as unambiguous evil. The result is an asymmetric moral counterweight that quietly shields Gandhi from the implications of the film’s own premise.

    Rather than allowing its critique of Gandhi as a father to stand on its own, the film drowns it in moralised packaging.

  3. From tragedy to hagiography: Instead of allowing the audience to contemplate the incompatibility between moral absolutism and family life, the film externalises blame onto society and Harilal himself, anyone but Gandhi. The question shifts from “What did Gandhi do to his son?” to the declaration that “Harilal simply failed to understand his father’s purpose.”

  4. The ending: The final sequence depicts Gandhi’s funeral, a rewritten Raghupati Raghav extolling Gandhi rather than Ram, and Harilal reduced to reaction shots. It is a near inversion of the film’s premise. The son’s tragedy is submerged beneath national mythmaking. The film does not reconcile private cost with public achievement; it declares the private cost justified and negligible.

Scoring

  1. Acting & Performances — 8
  2. Character Writing & Psychology — 5.5
  3. Narrative Focus & Structural Integrity — 3
  4. Screenplay & Dialogue — 7
  5. Cinematography & Visual Language — 9
  6. Direction & Tonal Control — 3.5
  7. Music & Sound — 7
  8. Thematic Honesty & Intellectual Integrity — 5
  9. Historical Handling — 5
  10. Ending & Emotional Resolution — 1

Final Average: 5.4 / 10
