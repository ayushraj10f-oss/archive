Nine Hours to Rama

1963
Director: Mark Robson
Writer: Stanley Mann (based on the novel by Stanley Wolpert)
Cast: Horst Buchholz, J.S. Casshyap, Valerie Gearon, Robert Morley

Nine Hours to Rama is a film about Gandhi's assassination, but the surprising part is that it is not morally instructive. Rather, it is extremely honest and politically serious. It is unambiguously pro-Gandhi, but never resorts to something typical of most Gandhi cinema: moralizing and hagiography.

Positives

  1. Performances

    Horst Buchholz delivers an excellent and intense performance as Nathuram Godse, making him equally appealing, sympathetic, and contemptible. Godse is portrayed as an energetic, fiery ideologue who becomes increasingly strained as he approaches Birla House. Buchholz conveys agitation, doubt, and internal pressure incredibly well, emphasizing physicality and expressiveness.

    J. S. Casshyap’s portrayal of Gandhi is absolutely astounding, especially for a virtually unknown actor in the mainstream. He plays Gandhi with gravity, restraint, moral loneliness, and a stubbornness that avoids typical theatrical reverence.

    Most supporting performances are solid. Don Borisenko’s Narayan Apte is understated and excellent, portraying wavering resolve and doubt well. Valerie Gearon, as Rani Mehta, is convincing and emotionally grounded despite the character being fictional. Two performances weaken the overall consistency and will be discussed later.

  2. Character Writing and Psychology

    This is the film’s greatest strength. Godse, the bogeyman of Indian politics, has been written with remarkable impartiality. He is neither reduced to comical villainy nor offered absolution. The film, admirably, never suggests that Godse is wavering in his beliefs; the tension comes from proximity to the actual act of assassination and the moral implications of that act.

    Gandhi is another extremely well-written character in the film: morally uncompromising, internally coherent, but exhausting to deal with. His refusal to adapt or accept protection is shown as a direct causal factor in his assassination. The film’s sanctification of Gandhi feels tragic rather than hagiographic.

  3. Thematic Honesty

    The film is evidently pro-Gandhi the person, but implicitly critical of Gandhianism as an ideology. The depiction of Hindu nationalism, its opposition to Gandhi, the RSS, and Golwalkar (as Prahlad) is nuanced, serious, and surprisingly sympathetic, even though the film fundamentally disagrees with it. It treats ideological opposition to Gandhi as coherent and justifiable rather than monstrous, and rejects assassination without collapsing into moral hysteria.

    Partition in particular, and Hindu–Muslim riots generally, are themes that hang over the entire film. Several characters are affected and shaped, directly or indirectly, by these events. Their portrayal is remarkably nuanced, depicted not as abstract communal hatred but as administrative collapse, elite failure, and unresolved rage.

  4. Cinematography

    The look of the film is very strong and evocative, and at times surprisingly innovative. It is also remarkably effective even in poor-quality prints. The visuals prioritize environment and narrative flow rather than spectacle.

  5. Music and Sound

    Subtle and effective, with particularly strong use of Indian musical elements. The score enhances tension without cueing moral judgment or sentimentality, which is typical of films of that era.

  6. Ending

    The ending is exceptionally well handled. Godse’s weeping regret, without ideological retreat, allows the tragedy to land without absolution or sermon. The film refuses emotional reassurance.

Negatives

  1. Pacing and Structure

    The film is noticeably longer than it needs to be. Some of the Rani Mehta flashbacks, in particular, overstay their welcome. The title evokes a linear, tight, and procedural narrative, which is not what the film offers. The flashbacks especially undermine the film’s structural premise.

  2. Secondary Performances

    Robert Morley’s PK Musaddi veers into caricature, and the portrayal of Superintendent Gopal Das by José Ferrer is wooden and underperformed. These are noticeable lapses in an otherwise competent ensemble.

Scoring

  1. Acting & Performances — 7.5
  2. Character Writing & Psychology — 9.5
  3. Narrative Focus & Structural Integrity — 6.0
  4. Screenplay & Dialogue — 7.5
  5. Cinematography & Visual Language — 8.5
  6. Music & Sound — 8.5
  7. Thematic Honesty & Intellectual Integrity — 9.0
  8. Historical Handling — 7.5
  9. Ending & Emotional Resolution — 9.5
  10. Pacing & Narrative Economy — 6.0

Final Average: 7.95 / 10
